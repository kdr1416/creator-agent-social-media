# Voice to Scene Design

Translate spoken meaning into a scene that can be pictured and built. This skill supplies the design bridge between a script and production. It is style- and renderer-independent: a separate style brief controls the look; the owning workflow controls research, audio, engine routing, implementation and rendering.

**Status:** initial design method, not yet validated across a film made from unseen narration. Examples illustrate reasoning, not approved templates or a promise of visual quality.

## Scope and useful inputs

Use the narration/transcript, nearby context, supplied timing, intended audience/format, style reference and available assets. Work with what exists. Distinguish known constraints from assumptions; ask only when missing information would materially change the design. A rough idea can have a rough timing plan; a build handoff needs sufficiently specific staging and timing.

If only an audio file is supplied, inspect/transcribe/align through the available media workflow; do not pretend to know its words or beat times. Preserve supplied voice and raw transcript. Proposed wording, corrections, merges or timing changes are separate editorial suggestions unless authorized.

Match the requested operation. Analysis can end with a diagnosis; a concept request can end with scene options; a design request produces a buildable plan. Creating this plan does not itself authorize assets, code, render, changes to existing handoff/status files, or publishing. When production is already authorized, pass the plan into that workflow and continue without an invented extra approval gate.

## A flexible design pass

These are thinking moves, not a fixed decision tree or a form to fill mechanically. Combine, revisit or omit a move when the material already answers it. Do not prescribe a universal scene count, number of alternatives, palette, mascot, layout, percentage, effect or timing formula.

### Read the meaning and its job in the story

Identify what the viewer should understand or feel after this beat. Consider the relationship being expressed, not only the nouns: examples include membership, sequence, contrast, cause, evidence, scale, uncertainty or relief. A sentence can serve several purposes; do not force a single label.

Check nearby lines when available. A sentence boundary need not create a new setting; one visual can develop over several lines, or one dense line can contain several phases. Keep fixed source slices intact unless the user authorizes changing them. Mark illustrative suggestions as such, and distinguish them from sourced factual claims.

**Meaning test:** would the proposed arrangement imply a relationship the voice never stated? Four subject areas do not automatically form a production line; an offer does not establish a scholarship or guarantee. Preserve the intended relationship even when using a metaphor.

### Find a visual proposition

Imagine a visible situation, comparison, reveal or transformation that carries the idea. State it in ordinary language: what is present, what attracts attention, and what changes? Avoid stopping at “use a map,” “add icons,” “show a student” or “make it dynamic.”

Use the idea's natural affordances. An environment, performed action, physical prop, document detail, spatial comparison, mechanism or typographic treatment may fit. These are lenses, not a menu that must be exhausted. A direct depiction can be better than an elaborate metaphor; a familiar arrangement can be better than novelty.

When the first idea feels generic or forced, consider a genuinely different staging and compare explanatory value, emotional tone, visual unity and feasibility. Do not generate alternatives just to satisfy a quota, or invent an unrelated story to make motion possible.

### Compose the frame before collecting objects

Choose the dominant image and the relationship of supporting elements to it. Establish an eye path, useful scale contrast, foreground/background or other depth cues where appropriate, and space for the coming change. Design the frame as a whole before assembling a list of independently decorated assets.

Explain why objects share a setting, surface, action, visual grouping or comparison. Whitespace and asymmetry can be deliberate; an unexplained empty region is not automatically elegance. A text heading does not always need the top-left corner, and each topic does not need its own isolated icon.

Use a protagonist when they contribute: they may act, inspect, guide, react or provide emotional continuity. They need not physically operate every concept. Do not add a character merely to fill a corner. Follow the chosen project's container, caption-safe-area and accessibility rules; this skill introduces no new layout quotas.

### Apply the chosen visual language

Carry the style's relationships into the new concept: palette roles and contrast, type treatment, line/shape language, asset mix, proportions, level of detail, spatial density and motion character. A shared background colour is not sufficient.

Separate reusable design grammar from the reference's specific content. Reuse a text treatment or visual hierarchy when it establishes continuity; do not repeat the same complete scene irrespective of meaning. With `warm-doodle-video`, for example, compact mascot reactions, vivid text strips and compatible cutout media can inform a new scene without importing the reference's street, school or product UI.

If several style interpretations remain plausible, state the proposed interpretation. Do not promote a rejected pilot, convenient rig or merely available font into a default. If no style was supplied, describe a coherent proposed direction as an assumption rather than pretending a preference was settled.

### Stage the beat over time

Describe the opening, the meaningful development and the result/hold in a form suited to the material. Locate changes against actual words or measured times when available. Reveal only what the viewer needs at that moment; account for reading time, transitions and the next scene.

Distinguish scene-level shifts of attention from object-level animation. A change of framing, revealed detail, shared action, annotation or new relationship may carry the beat. Fades, slides and bounces can support it; do not mistake a list of tweens for an explanation. Conversely, a clear quiet scene need not be forced into a complex mechanical transformation.

Fit the idea to the available duration. Shorten or simplify the visual argument before cramming more assets into a short voice slice. Do not invent second-level precision without audio evidence. Leave implementation choices such as easing curves to the animation stage unless they are essential to the idea.

### Check continuity and buildability

Consider how this scene belongs beside its neighbours: recurring subject, colour roles, framing rhythm, prop, action, sound cue if briefed, or another useful connection. Do not mechanically alternate engines or layouts merely for variety. Independently sampled scenes can demonstrate a style, but do not claim they prove a continuous film's flow.

Identify the few assets and independent layers that make the design possible. State whether they exist, need sourcing, or must be created. A reference image is evidence, not automatically a usable layered asset. Do not assume screenshots, character poses, licensed media, subject footage or a particular tool are available. If an asset is missing, propose a design-preserving alternative and disclose its trade-off.

Respect any locked engine routing. If the idea needs another engine, record the reason through the owning workflow rather than switching silently. No renderer, including HyperFrames, compensates for an unresolved visual concept.

## What to hand off

Use concise prose, a small storyboard table or the existing project's format. Do not create a competing giant schema. A useful design normally communicates:

- the source beat and intended takeaway;
- the visual proposition, opening composition and hierarchy;
- the visible development/result and its relation to spoken cues;
- the relevant style decisions, text treatment and character role if present;
- assets/layers, availability, timing assumptions and constraints;
- a neighbour handoff and any unresolved design risk when relevant.

Add coordinates, dimensions, references or pose detail when ambiguity would force the builder to invent the composition again. Those details can be relative (“the document dominates the upper-middle; mascot tracks its result below”) or measured, depending on the stage. Do not demand pixel coordinates for early ideation or leave a production packet at “make it playful.” Preserve required fields of the owning workflow rather than replacing them.

## Review the proposal before polishing it

Ask whether a viewer can infer the right relationship from the main image, whether the frame has a clear visual priority, whether the style survives beyond colour, and whether the development fits the time and assets. Also ask whether supporting elements are doing useful work. Record actual weaknesses and revise the premise or staging when needed, rather than adding effects to compensate.

Where authorized and useful, a key frame or rough motion test can expose problems before full production. This is a design aid, not a mandatory new user checkpoint. A written plan, technical pass and rendered style acceptance are different evidence; report which exists.

Read [reasoning examples](references/reasoning-examples.md) when a concept feels literal, when choosing among visual relationships, or when checking transfer to an unfamiliar topic. Examples are prompts for judgment, not mandatory templates.
